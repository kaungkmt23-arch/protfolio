01 — Origins

Born from the
same darkness
as the stars

The ocean floor and the cosmos share more than darkness — they share mystery. In the deepest trenches, where sunlight has never reached, life finds a way. Just as it does across the billion galaxies we have yet to explore.

"We know more about the surface of Mars than we do about our own ocean floor."

ABYSSAL exists at this intersection — between the impossibly deep and the impossibly vast. Our expeditions push the boundary of what is known.

02 — Discovery

Ancient giants of
the silent deep

Humpback whales navigate 25,000 kilometers of open ocean each year using a biological GPS we still don't fully understand. Their songs travel farther than any human signal we've ever built.

These leviathans have outlasted ice ages, mass extinctions, and five hundred million years of geological upheaval. They are living records of our planet's deepest history.

03 — Cosmos

Two frontiers,
one infinite
darkness

At 11,000 meters below sea level, the pressure is 1,100 times what you feel right now. At the edge of our observable universe, space stretches 46 billion light-years in every direction.

Both frontiers are defined by the absence of light, the presence of the unknown, and the certainty that something extraordinary waits in the dark.

The Cosmos Program
Our Story

A mission born
in the dark

ABYSSAL began not as a company, but as a question: what if the deep ocean and deep space are the same frontier — just measured in different directions?

Founded 2019 — Tokyo, Japan

The founding question

In 2019, marine biologist Dr. Sena Mori and astrophysicist Kenji Ishida met at a research symposium in Kyoto. Mori had spent seven years mapping hydrothermal vent ecosystems in the Mariana Trench. Ishida had spent six building adaptive optics for space telescopes. What brought them together was a shared obsession: that we were looking in the wrong direction.

"We kept spending billions to scan the sky," Ishida recalls, "when the most alien biology on Earth was sitting 11 kilometres beneath our feet, completely unstudied. The ocean floor is its own universe — darker, older, and in many ways stranger than anything we've found in space."

"The ocean floor is its own universe — darker, older, and in many ways stranger than anything we've found in space."

The first dive

Their first joint expedition in 2020 descended 6,200 metres into the Yap Trench, aboard a converted research submersible equipped with Ishida's modified deep-spectrum imaging sensors — originally designed to photograph distant galaxies, now retuned to read bioluminescent signal patterns in zero-light ocean environments.

What they recovered changed both their careers. The bioluminescent communication patterns observed in a newly-catalogued cephalopod species bore a structural resemblance to the pulsar timing signals Ishida had spent years analysing. Coincidence, most said. Possibility, they replied.

EXP-2020-01, Yap Trench, 6,200m — the first ABYSSAL dive. Photography: NEOM Archives.

What we do now

ABYSSAL now operates across three programmes: deep-sea biological survey, cross-disciplinary signal analysis, and public science communication. We partner with oceanographic institutes across Japan, Norway, and New Zealand, and maintain a fleet of four autonomous deep-sea vehicles capable of sustained operation below 8,000 metres.

Our Cosmos Protocol — launched in partnership with JAXA in 2024 — extends this comparison upward, using the International Space Station as a vantage point to study Earth's ocean surface dynamics alongside atmospheric phenomena. The data feeds directly back into our deep-sea modelling work.

We believe the most important discoveries of this century will not come from looking further into space, but from finally turning our attention downward — into the pressurised, lightless, ancient world that covers 71% of our planet and remains, in the most literal sense, unknown.
